01

Working paper

Cities with Invisible Walls: Labor Liberalization and Misallocation in China

With Kang Zhou

Revise & resubmit · Review of Economics and Statistics

Read abstract

We study the implications of migration barriers for labor misallocation by exploiting China’s hukou reform in the 2000s. The reform relaxed restrictions on rural migrant workers by granting them local hukou status. Employment increased by 26.0%, capital by 9.1%, and marginal revenue products of labor decreased by 30.2% for baseline high-productivity firms relative to low-productivity firms in treated prefectures compared with untreated ones. This suggests improved allocative efficiency as labor moved toward more productive firms. Efficiency gains were attenuated in later-reformed regions exposed to competition from early reform adopters. At the aggregate level, the reform increased productivity by 3.5% and manufacturing output by 6.48%.

05

Publication · 2026

Consumption Smoothing and Household Savings: Role of Demographics and Durables

With Neha Bairoliya, Areendam Chanda, and Fang Yang

Journal of Development Economics · Volume 184 · 103894

Read abstract

The canonical prediction of life-cycle models—that individuals smooth consumption over their lifetime—has mostly been tested in developed countries and has found little empirical support. We provide a developing-country perspective by examining life-cycle consumption, income, and savings in India. Unlike in the U.S., Indian households exhibit no growth in nondurable consumption after adjusting for family size. Saving for lumpy investments in consumer durables is a key driver of high savings rates and flat nondurable consumption over the life cycle.

06

Publication · 2024

On the Effectiveness of Insurance Mechanisms for the Older Individuals in China

With Yasuyuki Sawada

The Japanese Economic Review · Volume 75 · pp. 1007–1040

Read abstract

Does consumption smoothing indicate efficient insurance mechanisms for older individuals? Using China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study panel data from 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018, we examine institutional and personal insurance channels. Conventional tests support consumption smoothing against adverse health shocks, particularly for basic food consumption. Yet the welfare costs of these shocks can be substantial, especially in rural areas. The findings suggest that stronger long-term care, pension systems, and other social safety nets can improve welfare even when consumption appears insensitive to shocks.
